# Stage 4 Cross-Family Critic — Claude Sonnet 4.6 acting as adversarial reviewer

---

## L1-3 Chord

### Strongest case against
The half-completed-line locking mechanic turns this mode into a slow-motion grid seizure with no recoverable agency. Once you lay down a stripe that misses its column partner, that stripe permanently constrains every future placement — not as a puzzle element you can reason around, but as accumulated scar tissue from past decisions that compound arbitrarily. That is Scar disease wearing a new coat: the punishment is structural (locking), applied retroactively, and not addressable by any future clever move. More critically, the "10th play" test exposes the core emptiness: the decision question is always "can I complete both a row AND a column simultaneously?" — which collapses, on any given board state, into a binary yes/no spatial check. There is no depth ladder here. A player on play 10 asks the identical question as on play 1; the answer is just harder to compute because the board is noisier. The mode is also self-defeating at its win condition: to chord-clear you need to both complete a row and a column at once, but surviving without losing requires avoiding dead-end lock states, so the optimal strategy is extremely conservative placement that avoids partial lines altogether — meaning the mode's signature mechanic (chording) is penalised by its own loss condition, and a skilled player learns to play Classic-style with an asterisk rather than engage the chord system at all.

### Most likely failure mode at scale
By play 15–20, generated boards fill with stripe debris from near-misses, turning every new placement into a constraint-satisfaction puzzle over locked cells rather than the spatial creativity the mode advertises. At play 30+, edge-case instances where the tray offers no piece capable of simultaneously completing any row-column intersection are functionally unwinnable within the first tray — the generator has no mechanism to guarantee chord opportunities exist.

## L1-5 Color Chord Clear

### Strongest case against
This mode silently replaces one clear criterion (line full) with another (run of 4+ same-color) without generating any new strategic axis — it just makes the existing question harder to compute visually. The insight gap is the real killer: Classic asks "where does this piece fit to advance toward a full line?" and Color Chord asks "where does this piece fit to extend a same-color run?" — structurally identical questions, differing only in what pattern you're hunting. There is no dual-purpose tension because every placement is evaluated on exactly one dimension: does it extend or create a color run? The "negative space matters" ingredient fails completely; empty cells between same-color runs are inert — they don't count, they don't threaten, they just sit there, which means the board's empty space is invisible to strategy. The pre-fill goal (clear all pre-fill via color-runs) sounds like a puzzle constraint but is actually a soft impossibility guarantee problem: the generator must ensure that for each pre-fill color, 4+ contiguous cells can be assembled around it, but contiguous color runs are extremely sensitive to piece orientation and color assignment, meaning either the puzzles are trivially easy (pre-fill placed in obvious run starters) or unsolvable without backtracking that the player cannot perform. The color dimension also adds a layer of visual noise that substitutes for mechanical depth — players will feel like they're thinking about more things when they're actually thinking about less.

### Most likely failure mode at scale
Run-based clearing is highly susceptible to degenerate board states where pre-fill cells of the same color are isolated by opposing-color fills, making them structurally unreachable without a backtrack the mode doesn't support. By play 30, players who have internalized color-run strategy discover the optimal play is to ignore color diversity entirely and funnel one color into contiguous bands — collapsing the ostensibly rich color space into a single-color pipeline, which is both visually monotonous and strategically trivial.

## L1-6 Board Spin

### Strongest case against
The spin budget is a consumable resource grafted onto a spatial puzzle, and consumable resources in puzzle games always converge on one dominant strategy: hoard them until you are certain they are needed, then use them reactively. That is not a dual-purpose mechanic — it is a get-out-of-jail card with a price tag. The orientation-locked pieces (placed as dealt, no player rotation) look like added constraint, but in practice they are Pipeline disease: agency removed (piece rotation) without comparable agency added. Spinning the board is not equivalent agency to rotating a piece — it is categorically worse because it moves all existing fills simultaneously, creating ripple effects across the entire board that a player cannot fully compute before committing. The "order matters" and "negative space matters" ingredients therefore degrade into chaos management: players cannot anticipate what the board will look like after a spin because they are tracking 8×8 cells of prior fill plus a new piece, not a tractable local decision. The structural aha claimed here — "the board is the thing that moves, not the piece" — is a flavor insight, not a mechanical one; it doesn't generate new question types, it just reframes the same spatial question with higher cognitive cost. The spin budget also creates a meta-game where the player must evaluate spin-cost vs. board-state on every move even when not spinning, which is exhausting overhead that compounds rather than enriches.

### Most likely failure mode at scale
Spin opportunities are highest-value when the board is already dense, but dense boards combined with a 90° rotation routinely produce no-legal-placement situations that consume a spin budget move to escape — meaning the mode's resource is spent defensively rather than creatively, turning the puzzle into a stamina tax. At play 30, players who understand the system learn to pre-empt spins by maintaining artificially sparse boards, which means they're playing Classic with a spin timer, and the mode's signature mechanic is actively avoided for most of the run.

## L3-2 Twin Bond

### Strongest case against
This mode is Scar disease with extra steps. The scar (bond break → centroid scar) is not triggered by a player choosing to abandon a bond — it is triggered by a generator-determined bond assignment meeting a board state where no legal touching placement exists for the partner. That is a punishment determined by the intersection of random tray generation and board topology, neither of which the player fully controls. The "10th play" question is: "after placing the first bonded piece, is there a legal edge-adjacent cell for the partner?" — which is a spatial adjacency check, not a strategic decision. The bonded-pair halo is visible, the constraint is clear, but the agency is illusory: if the board has no legal touching placement for the partner, the player cannot create one. The lose condition (≥3 broken bonds per refill cycle) then means the player is playing against a random variable (are the bonded placements topologically compatible this cycle?) dressed up as skill. The "next placement MUST be the partner" constraint is also Pipeline disease in disguise — it removes the player's freedom to choose placement order across three slots without adding a commensurate new question type; it just forces a sequencing rail. The design would need the bond-break consequence to be player-caused (e.g., choosing to spend a resource to break the bond) rather than board-caused to escape Scar classification.

### Most likely failure mode at scale
Bond-break rate is a direct function of board density — as boards fill over a long run, the probability of no legal touching placement for a bond partner rises monotonically, meaning the mode's loss condition becomes increasingly luck-dependent rather than skill-dependent. By play 30, players on difficult generated instances will experience back-to-back bond breaks from irresolvable geometric constraints, not from strategic failure, and the mode will feel like a random death sentence.

## L7-3 Partitioned Board

### Strongest case against
Region-scoped clears sound like a new question but actually fragment the base game's clear mechanic into four simultaneous but independent sub-games. Each 4×4 region is its own Classic board with its own line-clear economy, and the 8×8 spatial field becomes an interface artifact rather than a meaningful game object. The anti-pattern here is subtle but fatal: because pieces can span region boundaries (an L-piece might cover cells in TL and TR), a single placement in the shared boundary column asks "does this complete a row-segment in TL, does it complete a row-segment in TR, how does it affect both regions' column fills?" — not because the design intended cross-region tension, but as an accidental side-effect of placing pieces on a grid that is visually unified but mechanically fragmented. This is Mirror disease: the region-clear rule is a function of an existing spatial decision (where you place the piece), not an independent constraint. The "dual-purpose mechanic" ingredient fails because no placement is ever genuinely dual-purpose — a cell is in exactly one region, and cross-boundary pieces are evaluated as separate contributions to separate regions, not as a unified strategic unit. The result is that the board's spatial field stops mattering as a whole; players mentally chop it into quarters and play four easy Classic games simultaneously, which is boring, not interesting. The "negative space matters" ingredient also fails: empty cells in one region are inert to the other three, so the board's large-scale topology is irrelevant.

### Most likely failure mode at scale
Because each 4×4 region clears independently, the long-run strategy is to fill regions unevenly — pack one region dense to trigger clears while keeping others sparse — which is a locally optimal strategy that the mode's rules do not penalise. At play 30, experienced players will have discovered this region-sequencing exploit and will be playing a degenerate one-region-at-a-time game that is strictly easier than Classic, at which point the mode offers no challenge and no novelty.

## L8-3 Hoard

### Strongest case against
The mode's core loop — clears generate pieces, pieces must be placed, running out of pieces ends the game — inverts the Classic scarcity relationship in a way that punishes efficiency. In Classic, more clears is unambiguously better; in Hoard, more clears generates more pieces, which extends your play time but does not directly advance the pre-fill goal, and may actively harm it by filling cells you need empty for the pre-fill target. This creates a confused incentive: do you clear aggressively (to get more pieces and more attempts at the pre-fill) or conservatively (to avoid generating pieces that will fill cells near the pre-fill target)? The confusion sounds like tension but is actually incoherence, because the optimal answer is always context-dependent in a way that is not visible from the game state — you cannot tell, from the board, whether clearing a given line will generate a piece that helps or hurts until the random piece is revealed. This is Scar disease at the strategic level: the consequence of a (good, line-clearing) action is a random piece that may be irrelevant or actively harmful. The pre-fill puzzle goal is also mechanically incompatible with the "survive N trays" framing — pre-fill requires targeted clearing of specific cells, but clear-based piece generation requires maintaining clearable line configurations, and these two goals compete for the same board space in ways that are frequently irresolvable without backtracking the mode doesn't support. The solvability guarantee ingredient is the hardest to satisfy here and is not addressed in the design: there is no mechanism to ensure that the seeded piece set can clear the pre-fill configuration given the specific clearing sequence available.

### Most likely failure mode at scale
Because the piece pool is seeded but clear-triggered, edge-case instances where the pre-fill configuration requires specific cell patterns that the available pieces cannot construct — given the board state created by clearing lines to generate pieces — are unsolvable but indistinguishable from solvable-but-hard instances. By play 30, players will have encountered dead-end states that are only recognizable as such in retrospect, creating a mode that feels arbitrary rather than fair.
